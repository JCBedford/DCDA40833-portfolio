Lab 4: Exploratory Data Analysis in Tableau

DCDA 40833 | Justus Bedford

Lab Synopsis

Assignment Requirements: For Lab 4, we conducted exploratory data analysis using Tableau Public. We were required to prepare our dataset, create multiple interactive visualizations (charts, maps, or dashboards), embed them in an HTML portfolio page, and write a detailed reflection covering data preparation, design choices, observed patterns, surprises, and an evaluation of Tableau as a tool.

Reflection and Analysis

Data Preparation

When I first uploaded the dataset into Tableau Public, I encountered several formatting issues. Some crime rate columns were automatically classified as text rather than numeric measures, which prevented me from creating histograms and properly sorting the top ten states in a bar chart. I manually adjusted the data types within the data source tab to ensure the fields were treated as numeric measures. I also reviewed column headers for consistency and filtered the dataset to focus on the most recent year when building the state comparison. After these adjustments, the dataset functioned correctly and allowed for smooth visualization development. Also, when posting my visualizations, I first made it into a dashboard, but I found that the dashboard was not working when I posted it, so I had to post the visualizations separately.

Design Choices

I created three primary visualizations to examine crime patterns from multiple analytical angles. First, I built a line graph showing the trend of crime rates over time. I used year as the dimension and crime rate as the measure in order to identify long-term increases or decreases. A line graph was the most appropriate choice because it clearly displays temporal change and allows for easy identification of peaks, declines, and overall trajectory.

In addition to the time trend, I created two bar charts to examine state-level differences. The first bar chart displays the top ten states with the highest violent crime rates. The second bar chart compares those same ten states using property crime rates. By holding the states constant and changing the measure, I was able to compare whether states with high violent crime also experience similarly high property crime. Together, these three visualizations move from national trends to state-level concentration and then to cross-crime comparison.

Expected Patterns

Based on prior knowledge of crime trends, I expected property crime rates to be higher than violent crime rates across states. The visualizations confirmed this expectation. I also anticipated the shape of the time trend - it is concurrent with the rise of the war on drugs, a prominent piece of history taught in many criminal justice courses. The line graph shows a significant increase in crime rates starting in the late 1970s, peaking in the early 1990s, and then declining steadily through the 2000s.

Surprises and Outliers

One notable surprise was the degree of variation within the top ten states. Although I expected differences, the gap between the highest and lowest states in that group was more substantial than anticipated. Additionally, the violent crime bar chart showed tighter clustering among most states than I initially expected, suggesting that extreme cases may receive disproportionate public attention compared to their statistical frequency.

Questions for Further Investigation

These patterns raise important questions. What socioeconomic or structural factors contribute to states that consistently rank highest in violent crime? How do reporting standards influence cross-state comparisons? Would incorporating variables such as poverty rates, urbanization levels, or educational attainment shift the interpretation of these patterns?

Tool Evaluation

I have used Tableau once before in my Data Analysis and Visualization course, and while I was able to use it in more detail this time, I still found it to be a user-friendly tool for creating interactive visualizations. Tableau Public's drag-and-drop interface allowed me to quickly test different measures and dimensions, while the dashboard feature enabled me to combine multiple visualizations into a cohesive presentation. However, data cleaning options are limited compared to tools like R or Python, and advanced statistical analysis is not easily integrated. Despite these limitations, Tableau excels at rapid pattern identification and professional presentation.

What I Learned & Skills Demonstrated

Lab 4 was where the course material became personally meaningful. Being able to visualize crime data - a topic central to my Criminal Justice major - using a professional analytics tool gave me a concrete sense of how data visualization translates into real-world analysis. I learned how to prepare messy data, choose appropriate chart types, and embed interactive content into a web page.

The process of troubleshooting Tableau's data type issues and the dashboard embedding problem also taught me patience and debugging skills that apply far beyond Tableau itself. Every tool has quirks; learning to work through them is part of becoming competent with technology.

This lab demonstrated my ability to clean and prepare data, create meaningful interactive visualizations in Tableau Public, embed those visualizations in a web page, and connect observed patterns to domain knowledge from Criminal Justice.
